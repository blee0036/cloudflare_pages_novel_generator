Chapter 1 The Long Road

The road out of the valley was never meant for carts. It had been cut by sheep and widened by men who were in a hurry, and every spring the rain took back whatever the summer had flattened. Harrow walked it anyway, because the alternative was the ferry, and the ferry cost money he did not have.

He carried a canvas bag with two shirts, a tin cup, and a letter he had read so often that the folds had gone soft. The letter said very little. It gave a name, a town, and a date that had already passed by eleven days. Harrow had decided not to think about the eleven days until he was close enough for the thinking to be useful.

By noon the valley was behind him. The hills here were bare on top and wooded in the folds, and the wind came across them in long slow pushes that smelled of wet stone. He stopped at a milepost, sat on it, and ate half of the bread he had brought. The other half he wrapped again, because a man who eats all his bread on the first day is a man who has already decided to turn back.

A cart passed him in the afternoon, going the other way. The driver nodded but did not slow down, and Harrow did not raise a hand. There was a rhythm to walking that he had only just found, and stopping to be friendly would have cost him an hour of it.

The light went early behind the ridge. He found a dry hollow under a leaning rock, built a fire that was more smoke than heat, and sat with his back against the stone until the stars came out hard and close. Somewhere below him a stream was working at a bank, patient and unhurried, taking a handful of the hill away each night.

Chapter 2 A Letter from the North

The town was smaller than the letter had made it sound. It had one street that mattered and three that did not, a mill that had stopped running in his grandfather's time, and an inn whose sign had been repainted so often that the horse on it now looked like a dog.

Harrow asked for the name in the letter at the inn, at the mill, and finally at the smithy, where a woman with burned forearms told him that the man he wanted had gone north in the autumn and had left no word about coming back. She said it kindly, which made it worse. Then she asked whether he could work, and when he said he could, she pointed at a pile of scrap and told him to sort it by thickness.

He worked there for nine days. The pay was food and a corner of the loft, and on the fourth evening the smith's wife added a small coin without comment, as if it had always been part of the arrangement. Harrow kept the coin in the same pocket as the letter, where he could feel both of them at once.

On the ninth night a courier came through with post for the whole valley, and among the bundle was a second letter in the same hand as the first. It had been written six weeks earlier and had travelled by a route that no one could explain. It said that the writer was well, that the winter had been hard but not cruel, and that he should not come north until the passes opened.

Harrow read it twice by the forge light and then a third time in the loft. He understood that he had walked eleven days to be told to wait, and that waiting in this town was no worse than waiting anywhere else. In the morning he asked the smith whether there was more scrap.

Chapter 3 The Ferry at Dawn

The passes opened late that year. Harrow heard about it from a drover who had come up from the south with forty sheep and a great deal of opinion, and who described the thaw as though he had arranged it himself.

He left the town on a Tuesday with a better bag, better boots, and a second coin. The smith walked him to the end of the street, which was as far as anyone in that town walked with anybody, and said that if the north disappointed him there would be scrap to sort in the autumn. Harrow said he would remember, and he meant it.

The ferry he had avoided in the spring was the only way across the flood. He reached it at dusk and slept in the reeds so as to be first in the queue, and at dawn he handed over one of his two coins and stepped onto boards that moved under him like something alive. The river was brown and wide and moving faster than it looked, and the ferryman worked the rope with the unhurried disgust of a man who has done the same thing ten thousand times.

Halfway across, the light came over the eastern hills all at once. The water turned the colour of weak tea, the far bank resolved into trees and a road and a low white building, and Harrow found that he was holding the rail harder than he needed to. He let go of it, one finger at a time, and watched the bank come up to meet him.

Chapter 4 Homecoming

He found the house in the third week of summer. It was smaller than the letter had suggested, which by then he had come to expect, and the door was open because the day was hot.

They talked until the light failed, and then they talked in the dark, and neither of them mentioned the eleven days.
